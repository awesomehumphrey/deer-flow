# Anti-Cliché System

The narrative-nonfiction skill's freshness enforcement system. Cliché prose is a significant failure mode in AI-generated non-fiction. This document defines what to avoid and how to replace it.

## Core Principle

**Cliché = the first thing that comes to mind.** Fresh prose requires going past the obvious to find the specific, the surprising, the evidence-backed. In non-fiction, clichés are particularly damaging because they undermine the author's credibility.

---

## Banned Phrase Categories

### 1. Opening Clichés
| Banned | Why | Fresh Alternative Pattern |
|--------|-----|--------------------------|
| "In today's world..." | Vague and universal; nothing specific | Start with a concrete moment, statistic, or encounter |
| "It is often said that..." | Anonymised claim; who says it? | Attribute specifically or cut entirely |
| "Since the dawn of time..." | Historical cliché; never specific | Ground in a particular historical moment |
| "We live in an age of..." | Trendy but empty | Name the specific age, era, or condition |
| "The question of our time..." | Overclaiming without evidence | Let the evidence build the stakes |

### 2. Argument Clichés
| Banned | Why | Fresh Alternative Pattern |
|--------|-----|--------------------------|
| "It goes without saying..." | Lazy emphatic; often contradicts itself | Show why it matters through evidence |
| "It is important to note..." | Mechanical transition; adds nothing | Let the evidence speak |
| "At the end of the day..." | Management-speak filler | State the conclusion directly |
| "The fact of the matter is..." | Empty assertion | Present the fact |
| "Needless to say..." | Foregone conclusion without substance | Either say it or don't |

### 3. Evidence Clichés
| Banned | Why | Fresh Alternative Pattern |
|--------|-----|--------------------------|
| "Studies have shown..." | Vague attribution; which studies? | Name specific studies, researchers, or sources |
| "It is widely accepted that..." | Who accepts it? Be specific | Name the consensus or show the disagreement |
| "Experts agree..." | Which experts? | Attribute to named experts |
| "Research suggests..." | Passive and anonymous | Show who did the research |
| "Evidence points to..." | Abstract evidence | Present the specific evidence |

### 4. Emotional Clichés
| Banned | Why | Fresh Alternative Pattern |
|--------|-----|--------------------------|
| "A chilling example..." | Telling rather than showing | Let the example speak for itself |
| "A stark warning..." | Overwrought | Let the data be the warning |
| "A wake-up call..." | Overused activist language | Show what should wake the reader |
| "The human cost..." | Abstract when it should be concrete | Name the specific humans |
| "A troubling picture..." | Vague judgment | Describe what specifically is troubling |

### 5. Description Clichés
| Banned | Why | Fresh Alternative Pattern |
|--------|-----|--------------------------|
| "The digital age..." | Generic | Name the specific technological or social shift |
| "In the era of..." | Empty periodisation | Ground in specific dates or events |
| "Silicon Valley..." | Stereotyped | Be specific about which companies, which people |
| "The tech industry..." | Homogenising | Name the specific actors |
| "Big data..." | Jargon without meaning | Explain what the data actually is |

### 6. AI-Specific Clichés (HIGHEST PRIORITY TO AVOID)
| Banned | Why | Fresh Alternative Pattern |
|--------|-----|--------------------------|
| "A tapestry of..." | AI's favourite metaphor | Name the specific pattern |
| "The complex landscape..." | Corporate-speak emptiness | Describe the actual landscape |
| "In an increasingly digital world..." | Trendy but meaningless | Be specific about the digital shift |
| "At the intersection of..." | Jargon of false depth | Name the actual intersection |
| "The ecosystem..." | Tech-speak | Name the actual relationships |
| "Moving the needle..." | Management-speak | Quantify the impact |
| "A paradigm shift..." | Overused since the 1990s | Describe what actually changed |
| "Disruptive technology..." | jargon | Explain the specific disruption |
| "Leveraging..." | Corporate-speak | Say what is actually being done |
| "End-to-end..." | Tech jargon without meaning | Explain the actual process |

## Freshness Rules

### Rule 1: The Specificity Test
**If you could say it about any topic in any book, it is too generic.** Replace with something only THIS author, writing about THIS subject, would say.

- GENERIC: "The technology industry faces significant challenges."
- SPECIFIC: "Apple, Google, and Meta face regulatory challenges that differ by geography but share a common source: their advertising models."

### Rule 2: The Attribution Test
**Every claim needs a source.** Anonymous citations undermine credibility.

- WEAK: "Studies have shown that..."
- STRONG: "Chen et al. (2023) found that..."

### Rule 3: The Evidence Test
**Never assert what you can demonstrate.** Show the evidence, then let the reader draw the conclusion.

- TOLD: "This is a serious problem."
- SHOWN: "In 2024, the FTC received 2.1 million reports of fraud, a figure that represents a 20% increase on the previous year."

### Rule 4: The Person Test
**Abstract harm is always weaker than specific human stories.** When discussing harm, ground it in particular people.

- ABSTRACT: "Users are harmed by dark patterns."
- CONCRETE: "Sarah, a 67-year-old retiree from Ohio, was charged £847 for a subscription she never intended to start."

### Rule 5: The Analogy Test
**If an analogy has been used in a thousand articles, find a new one.** Original analogies come from the author's specific research and thinking.

- DEAD: "Like a digital Wild West..."
- ALIVE: "The app store ecosystem functions like a medieval guild system: the platform owner sets the rules, extracts the toll, and punishes deviation."

### Rule 6: The Verb Test
**Strong verbs beat weak verb + adverb combinations.** Replace passive constructions with active ones.

- WEAK: "The problem is being addressed..."
- STRONG: "Regulators are addressing the problem..."

## Academic-to-Narrative Translation

Non-fiction often involves translating academic or technical material into accessible prose. Avoid these translation clichés:

| Academic Cliché | Why | Narrative Alternative |
|----------------|-----|---------------------|
| "This paper argues that..." | Mechanical academic framing | Lead with the argument itself |
| "The literature suggests..." | Anonymous academic consensus | Cite specific researchers or findings |
| "It can be observed that..." | Passive and empty | Describe what can be observed |
| "As previous research has demonstrated..." | Lazy back-referencing | Reference specific prior work |
| "Methodologically..." | Jargon | Explain the method in plain language |
| "These findings indicate..." | Hedging when you mean "prove" | Use stronger language when warranted |

---

## Freshness Verification Checklist

Before any prose output, verify:

- [ ] No phrases from the Banned lists appear
- [ ] Every claim is attributed to a specific source
- [ ] Evidence is shown, not just asserted
- [ ] Abstract concepts are grounded in specific examples
- [ ] Analogies are original to this book
- [ ] Strong, active verbs are used throughout
- [ ] No AI-specific patterns detected
- [ ] Academic material has been translated into accessible prose
- [ ] Opening hooks are concrete, not generic
- [ ] Each paragraph contains at least one surprising detail or piece of evidence
